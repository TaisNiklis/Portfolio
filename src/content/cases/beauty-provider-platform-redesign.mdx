## TL;DR
The provider side of a beauty and healthcare booking platform **looked like a prototype and treated a freelancer and a clinic chain the same way**. The client had a limited budget. Our audit of 53 findings **convinced them to fund a redesign**, which we split into flows delivered in parts. I delivered **a new design system and six core flows** where each provider type gets only what it needs. The client's developers are now finishing the first scope.

## Context
The ecosystem has a **customer mobile app** for booking and a **provider web platform** for managing services, specialists, branches and the business profile.

- **A limited budget.** We could not fix everything the audit found.
- **No extra development cost.** Features that need new backend work wait for later phases.

## The problem
**Business problem:** providers are the supply side of the marketplace. If setting up services, specialists and branches is hard, customers in the app see fewer and weaker listings. [TODO: did the client name a business reason?]

**User problem:** the interface **looked like a low-fidelity prototype**: grey, generic icons, bold text everywhere. Labels changed between the menu and the page ("Appointments" vs. "Schedule"), and some steps were dead ends, such as a verification screen with no "Back" or "Resend code".

**How might we give every type of provider one clear way to manage their business, within a budget that allows only a first phase?**

## Goals
- A design system that makes the platform consistent.
- Clear flows for services, specialists, branches and the business profile.
- **One structure for a freelancer, a salon and a multi-location business**, with room to grow.

## My role
- **Ran the audit with another designer**; we reviewed each other's findings.
- **Presented the audit**, which became the main reason the client agreed to the redesign.
- **Split the redesign into flows** and ordered them by the client's priorities and budget.
- **Designed the provider-type model, six core flows and the design system.**
- **Was the client's main contact**: presented designs, argued for extra features, handled revisions.

## Discovery & insights
The audit was a **heuristic evaluation of the full provider journey**, from sign-up to branches. **Each of its 8 sections ends with a priority matrix: critical, high, medium / low.** The result: a 62-page report with 53 findings.

<Figure src="[TODO: audit-findings.png]" caption="One audit finding and a section priority matrix. The priorities became the basis for the redesign order." />

1. **There was no visual system.** Text contrast fell to **1.08:1** on the verification field and **2.29–2.81:1** on labels, against the 4.5:1 WCAG minimum. → Build the palette, type scale and component states first.
2. **The same task worked differently in each section.** Some sections added items on full pages, others had their own uploaders. → One set of patterns for all management areas.
3. **The structure did not support growth.** A specialist could not become a salon without a new account, **losing reviews and clients**. → A model where a freelancer can grow into a salon or clinic.
4. **New providers started from an empty screen** with no first action. → Every empty state explains the next step.

## Key decisions
### The audit as the case for a redesign
The client came with a limited budget, not a redesign plan. **The audit made the case**: the client reacted positively, and it became the critical factor in their decision.

### Delivering by flow
53 findings were more than the budget could cover. **We split the redesign into flows and delivered them in parts.** The order followed the client's priorities, and for each budget part I looked for the way to cover **the largest number of flows**. Appointments and Customers stayed in the backlog.

### Where I pushed for more, and what we cut
- **Financial tracking.** I recommended it as the data providers care about most. It needed extra backend work, so **the client declined it for now**.
- **Payments between provider and customer.** I proposed the logic; the client decided it was not a priority yet.
- **Monetisation.** The client wanted it, but **the core logic had to be agreed first**, with paid flows on top later.

### Hide what a provider does not need
Before, **business types were neither different in the interface nor verified**. I compared two options: one full interface with hints and empty states, or one interface that hides what a type does not need. **I chose hiding**, because most providers have little experience with complex software. A freelancer has no Specialists or Branches; instead, **the profile explains what to do when the business grows** and lets them switch to a salon or clinic.

Two things stayed simple on purpose: **specialists are linked to services manually** (the client's choice, to keep development cheaper), and **a full specialist-to-salon transfer went to the backlog**, because specialists have no platform access in version one.

## The solution
### 1. Sign-up that knows the business type
**Before:** types without descriptions, a 3-step indicator that opened the product after step 1, a verification dead end.

**After:** the provider picks **Salon, Clinic or Freelancer, then in-salon, home service or both**. The documents asked for depend on the type; a clinic must add a healthcare license. Recovery got a **60-second resend timer** and password rules that turn green as they are met, and a pop-up explains the review status after sign-up.

<Figure src="[TODO: signup-business-type.png]" caption="Business type and service location in one step. The answer sets the documents and the navigation the provider gets." />

### 2. A dashboard that is never empty
**Before:** table headers without data and no first action.

**After:** four stat cards with a direct action each, filters, today's appointments, top services and specialists' workload. **In the empty state, every block says what will appear and offers the next step.** A freelancer's dashboard drops branches and specialists.

<Figure src="[TODO: dashboard-before-after.png]" caption="Dashboard before / after: from empty tables to stat cards, filters and today's work." />

<Figure src="[TODO: dashboard-empty-freelancer.png]" caption="Empty state with a next step in every block, and the freelancer version." />

### 3. One pattern for services, specialists and branches
**I chose a side drawer over full pages** for every add and edit form: the list stays visible, multi-step forms get space and a sticky footer, and the pattern scales across the product. All three sections share search, filters, bulk actions and a context menu.

- **Services:** fixed categories with counters, own subcategories with drag-and-drop. A service no specialist can perform is hidden, with an icon that says why.
- **Specialists:** services assigned in a pop-up, and a share link that books this specialist.
- **Branches:** a 3-step drawer, cards for the top branch by bookings and the highest-rated one. **"Delete" became "Archive"**, as the audit recommended.

<Figure src="[TODO: services-before-after.png]" caption="Services before / after: categories, search, filters and bulk actions on one screen." />

<Figure src="[TODO: branch-drawer.png]" caption="Add Branch in a side drawer: the list stays in view, the form gets 3 clear steps." />

### 4. A profile that grows with the business
Two tabs: **Business & Account** (documents with a verification status) and **Public Profile** (what customers see). **The profile cannot go public until services, schedule, location and specialists are set**, and every missing part has an alert icon. A banner warns three days before a document expires. **A freelancer can change the business type** in a pop-up that lists the licenses each type needs.
