## TL;DR
A service-booking ecosystem connects customers with beauty and healthcare providers. Its provider web platform **looked inconsistent and had to work for very different businesses**: a freelancer, a salon and a clinic chain with several branches. The client had a limited budget. Our audit of 53 findings **convinced the client to commit to a redesign**. We split it into flows delivered in parts, and I delivered **a new visual direction, a design system and six core flows**. The client's developers are now finishing the first scope.

## Context
The ecosystem has a **customer mobile app** for booking and a **provider web platform** for managing services, specialists, branches and the business profile.

Constraints:
- **A limited client budget.** We could not fix everything the audit found.
- **No extra development cost.** The redesign changes how the product looks and is organised; new features need extra backend work and wait for later phases.

## The problem
**Business problem:** the provider platform is the supply side of the marketplace. If providers find it hard to set up services, specialists and branches, customers in the app see fewer and weaker listings. [TODO: did the client name a business reason — new providers, complaints, sales?]

**User problem:** the interface **looked like a low-fidelity prototype**: grey, generic icons, bold text everywhere. Similar tasks worked differently across sections, labels changed between the menu and the page ("Appointments" vs. "Schedule"), and some steps were dead ends, such as a verification screen with no "Back" or "Resend code".

**How might we give every type of provider one clear way to manage their business, within a budget that allows only a first phase?**

## Discovery & insights
The audit was a **heuristic evaluation of the full provider journey**: sign-up, dashboard, profile, appointments, customers, services, specialists and branches. **Each section ends with a priority matrix: critical, high, medium / low.** Another designer and I reviewed each other's findings. The result: a 62-page report with 53 findings.

<Figure src="[TODO: audit-findings.png]" caption="One audit finding and a section priority matrix. The priorities became the basis for the phase-one scope." />

1. **There was no shared visual system.** Text contrast was as low as **1.08:1** on the verification field and **2.29–2.81:1** on labels and inputs, against the 4.5:1 WCAG minimum. The CTA colour was also used on secondary elements. → Build the palette, type scale and component states first.
2. **The same task worked differently in each section.** Services and specialists opened full pages for adding items, and the service form had its own image uploader. → Reuse one set of patterns across all management areas.
3. **The structure did not support growth.** A specialist could not upgrade to a salon account and had to start a new one, **losing reviews, services and clients**. Business types at sign-up had no descriptions. → Design one entity model where a freelancer can grow into a salon or a chain.
4. **New providers started from an empty screen.** The setup showed 3 steps but opened the product after step 1. Dashboard, customers and branches had no first action. → Every empty state explains the next step.
5. **Entities were shown in isolation.** A specialist profile listed every salon service, not their own. → Show relationships: specialist ↔ services, branch ↔ specialists.

## Exploring options
### The audit as the case for a redesign
The client came with a limited budget, not a redesign plan. **The audit made the case:** the client reacted positively, and it became the critical factor in their decision to fund the redesign.

### Delivering by flow, not all at once
53 findings were more than the budget could cover. Instead of one big release, **we split the redesign into flows and delivered them in parts**. The order followed the client's priorities, and for each budget part I looked for the way to cover **the largest number of flows**. The rest of the audit, **including the full Appointments and Customers sections**, stayed as a ready backlog.

### Where I pushed for more, and what we cut
- **Financial tracking.** I recommended adding financial data to the dashboard, because revenue is the number providers care about most. It needed extra backend work, so **the client declined it for now**.
- **Payments between provider and customer.** I proposed the payment logic; the client decided it was not a priority yet.
- **Monetisation.** The client wanted it, but it had to wait: **first finish the redesign and agree the core logic**, then add paid flows on top.

### One entity model for three provider types
Before the redesign, **business types were neither different in the interface nor verified**: a freelancer and a clinic chain saw the same product. I compared two options:

- **One full interface for everyone**, with hints and empty states. A freelancer would see sections like "Specialists" that make no sense for one person.
- **One interface that hides what a provider type does not need.**

**I chose to hide extra pages over showing everything with hints**, because most providers have little experience with complex software. A freelancer has no "Specialists" section. Instead, **the profile shows a hint on what to do when the business grows**, and the freelancer can switch the profile type to a salon or clinic.

The model links four entities: **business profile → branches → specialists → services**. A salon or clinic adds specialists; a multi-location business adds branches, each with its own specialists and services.

Two decisions stayed simple on purpose:
- **Specialists are linked to services manually.** The audit suggested filtering services by profession; the client chose manual linking to keep development cheaper.
- **A full specialist-to-salon transfer went to the backlog.** In the first version specialists have no access to the platform, so only the profile type switch was needed.

## What I learned
- **An audit can sell the project.** A clear, prioritised report turned a limited budget into a funded redesign.
- **Delivering by flow protects the budget.** The client always knew what the next part would cost and bring.
- **Model the entities before the screens** when one product serves different business types.
- **Next:** I would bring the financial overview and provider payments back as the first items for phase two, and measure how many freelancers switch to a salon profile.

> **AI in my process:** I used AI to search for visual style references, to draft user flows, and to format the audit text into a consistent report. The findings, the priorities, the scope and the final design decisions stayed with us.
